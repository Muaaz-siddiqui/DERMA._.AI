VASCULAR LESIONS

What are Vascular Lesions?
Vascular lesions are abnormalities of blood vessels in or near the skin. They include a wide range of conditions from simple birthmarks to more complex vascular malformations. Most vascular lesions are benign but some can indicate underlying health conditions. They appear in various colors including red pink purple and blue due to the blood vessels visible through the skin.

Types of Vascular Lesions:

Hemangiomas:
- Infantile hemangiomas: Most common vascular tumor in infants, appear after birth and usually resolve on their own
- Cherry angiomas: Small bright red spots common in adults over 30, completely benign
- Spider angiomas: Small blood vessels radiating from central point, can indicate liver disease

Vascular Malformations:
- Port wine stains: Flat pink to dark red birthmarks caused by abnormal capillaries, do not fade with age
- Venous malformations: Blue soft compressible lesions caused by abnormal veins
- Arteriovenous malformations: Abnormal connections between arteries and veins, can be serious
- Lymphatic malformations: Abnormal lymph vessels, appear as soft swellings

Telangiectasias:
- Small dilated blood vessels near skin surface
- Appear as fine red or purple lines
- Can occur on face legs and other areas
- Associated with rosacea aging sun damage and some systemic diseases

Purpura and Petechiae:
- Small red or purple spots caused by bleeding under skin
- Petechiae: Tiny pinpoint spots
- Purpura: Larger spots
- Can indicate bleeding disorders or vasculitis requiring medical evaluation

Appearance:
- Red pink purple or blue discoloration of skin
- Flat or raised lesions
- Can be small pinpoint or cover large areas
- Some blanch (turn white) when pressed, others do not
- May be present at birth or develop later in life
- Some pulsate or feel warm due to blood flow

Causes:
- Congenital abnormalities in blood vessel development
- Sun damage causing telangiectasias
- Hormonal changes during pregnancy
- Aging causing cherry angiomas
- Liver disease causing spider angiomas
- Genetic conditions like hereditary hemorrhagic telangiectasia
- Trauma causing bruising and purpura
- Inflammatory conditions

Risk Factors:
- Family history of vascular lesions
- Sun exposure causing spider veins and telangiectasias
- Pregnancy and hormonal changes
- Aging
- Certain medications affecting blood vessels
- Liver disease
- Underlying bleeding or clotting disorders

When Vascular Lesions Are Concerning:
Most vascular lesions are harmless but some require medical evaluation:
- Rapidly growing vascular lesion especially in infants
- Lesion that bleeds repeatedly
- Lesion causing functional problems due to location
- Multiple spider angiomas suggesting liver disease
- Purpura that does not blanch which can indicate vasculitis or meningococcemia
- Lesion with symptoms like pain warmth or ulceration
- Any vascular lesion you are unsure about

Treatment Options:
Many vascular lesions do not require treatment but options include:
- Laser therapy: Most effective for port wine stains telangiectasias and cherry angiomas
- Pulsed dye laser: Specifically designed for vascular lesions
- Sclerotherapy: Injection to collapse abnormal vessels
- Surgical excision: For some vascular malformations
- Propranolol: For infantile hemangiomas requiring treatment
- Embolization: For arteriovenous malformations
- Observation: For stable benign lesions

When to See a Doctor:
- Any new vascular lesion you are unsure about
- Rapidly growing lesion
- Lesion that bleeds ulcerates or causes pain
- Multiple new telangiectasias or spider angiomas
- Purpura or petechiae especially if widespread or not from injury
- Vascular lesion in a child that is growing rapidly
- Any lesion affecting vision breathing or other functions due to location

Important Note:
While most vascular lesions are benign some can indicate underlying systemic conditions. Always have new or changing vascular lesions evaluated by a dermatologist. Never attempt to treat vascular lesions at home. Purpura that does not blanch can be a medical emergency requiring immediate attention. Always consult a qualified dermatologist or vascular specialist for proper diagnosis and treatment of vascular lesions.
