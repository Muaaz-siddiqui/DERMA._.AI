BASAL CELL CARCINOMA (BCC)

What is Basal Cell Carcinoma?
Basal cell carcinoma is the most common form of skin cancer and the most frequently occurring cancer overall. It begins in the basal cells which are found at the bottom of the epidermis (outer skin layer). Basal cell carcinoma grows slowly and rarely spreads to other parts of the body but it can be locally destructive if not treated early. It most commonly appears on sun-exposed areas especially the face, head, and neck.

Appearance:
- Pearly or waxy bump often with visible blood vessels
- Flat flesh-colored or brown scar-like lesion
- Bleeding or scabbing sore that heals and returns
- Pink growth with raised edges and crusted center
- White waxy lesion that looks like a scar
- Shiny skin-colored bump that may be translucent
- Can appear pink red white brown or black depending on skin tone

Types of Basal Cell Carcinoma:
- Nodular BCC: Most common type, appears as round pearly bump
- Superficial BCC: Appears as flat scaly reddish patch
- Morpheaform BCC: Looks like a scar, most aggressive type
- Pigmented BCC: Has brown black or blue color
- Infiltrative BCC: Grows between tissue fibers making it hard to detect

Causes:
- Ultraviolet radiation from sunlight
- Tanning bed use
- Radiation therapy for other conditions
- Prolonged arsenic exposure
- Immune suppression from medications or disease
- Rare inherited conditions like basal cell nevus syndrome

Risk Factors:
- Chronic sun exposure
- Fair skin with light eyes and hair
- Personal or family history of skin cancer
- Age over 50 though younger people can also get it
- Living in high altitude or sunny climate
- Previous radiation therapy
- Weakened immune system

Why It Needs Treatment:
Although basal cell carcinoma rarely spreads to other organs it can cause significant local destruction. Untreated BCC can invade surrounding tissues including bone and nerves causing serious damage especially on the face. It can also recur after treatment. Early treatment leads to excellent outcomes with cure rates above 95 percent.

Treatment Options:
- Mohs surgery: Gold standard treatment, highest cure rate, preserves healthy tissue
- Excision: Surgical removal with margin of healthy tissue
- Curettage and electrodesiccation: Scraping and burning the tumor
- Cryotherapy: Freezing with liquid nitrogen for superficial BCC
- Radiation therapy: For patients who cannot have surgery
- Topical medications: Imiquimod or fluorouracil for superficial BCC
- Targeted therapy: Hedgehog pathway inhibitors for advanced cases

When to See a Doctor Immediately:
- Any non-healing sore that bleeds or crusts repeatedly
- Pearly or shiny bump on face head or neck
- Any new growth that looks unusual
- Sore that looks like a scar but you have no injury there
- Any skin change in a person with history of sun damage
- Growth that continues to change or grow

Prevention:
- Apply broad spectrum SPF 30 or higher sunscreen daily
- Wear protective clothing hats and sunglasses
- Avoid sun exposure between 10am and 4pm
- Never use tanning beds
- Perform monthly self skin exams
- Annual skin checks by a dermatologist especially if high risk

Important Note:
Basal cell carcinoma is cancer and requires professional medical evaluation and treatment. If you notice any suspicious skin lesion especially on sun-exposed areas see a dermatologist immediately. Early detection and treatment result in excellent outcomes. Do not ignore persistent non-healing sores on your skin.
